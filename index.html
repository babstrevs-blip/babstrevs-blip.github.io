Five things I can help with.

The crossed-out line is how the policy says it. Under that is what it actually means, and then the part that usually doesn't make it into the sales pitch.

Term life

Level premium term life, guaranteed renewable and convertible, coverage terminating at the end of the level period.

A set amount of money for your family if you die, for a set number of years, at a price that can't go up during those years.

Straight up: this gets you the most coverage for the least money. If it runs out and you're still alive, you don't get anything back. That's the trade, and for most young families it's still the right one.

Whole life

Participating whole life with guaranteed cash value accumulation and non-forfeiture options.

Coverage that doesn't expire as long as you keep paying, and that slowly builds up a cash value you can borrow against later.

Straight up: it costs several times more than term for the same payout. It's the right answer for some people and oversold to a lot of others. I'll tell you which one you are.

Indexed universal life

Flexible premium adjustable life with an index-linked interest crediting strategy subject to participation rates, caps, and floors.

Permanent coverage where the cash value grows based on a market index, with a ceiling on the good years and a floor so you don't lose value in the bad ones.

Straight up: this is the most complicated thing on the page and the one most often sold off a best-case illustration. If anyone shows you one, ask to see the guaranteed column and not the projected one. I'll show you both.

Living benefit riders

Accelerated death benefit provisions for terminal, chronic, and critical illness, payable as a discounted advance of the face amount.

An add-on that lets you pull part of your own death benefit out early if you get diagnosed with something serious. Meaning the policy can help you while you're still alive.

Straight up: this is often included at no extra cost and almost never mentioned. If you already have a policy through work or anywhere else, go find out whether you have this. That one's free advice.

Annuities

A deferred annuity contract providing tax-deferred accumulation and an optional guaranteed lifetime withdrawal benefit, subject to a surrender charge schedule.

You hand an insurance company a chunk of money, it grows without being taxed each year, and later it pays you back either in a lump sum or as a check every month for as long as you live.

Straight up: the tradeoff is access. Your money is locked up for a set number of years and pulling it out early costs you a surrender charge. If there's any chance you need that money soon, an annuity is the wrong tool. If it's money you were never going to touch until retirement anyway, that's when it starts making sense.
